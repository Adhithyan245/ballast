    How the gauge was built

    A preregistered study first, then a scorer trained on what it found

    Ballast is not a new biomedical model. It reads how an answer behaves under repetition, quoting and pressure, signals the study showed to matter.

    Measured 8,998

    answers from five model families on 100 expert-labeled PubMedQA questions, collected under a plan frozen before the first call.

    Claude and Gemini repeated the same answer on 89–92% of questions, yet switched to a user's suggestion after one "Are you sure?" on 29–51% of them. With the abstract, that switching fell by more than half.

    Learned

    A small logistic model trained on 800 of the study's cases (4 models × 2 settings × 100 questions) to predict when the majority answer is wrong. Tested only on questions it never saw:

    AUROC: the chance a wrong answer scores higher risk than a right one (50% is guessing). Predicted risk tracked observed error: 17%→22%, 29%→27%, 49%→53%, 74%→69%.

    Limits
    • Not clinical advice. Correctness here means agreement with PubMedQA's expert labels.
    • Trained on 100 questions; the risk number is a guide, not a guarantee.
    • The quote check confirms a quote exists, not that it supports the answer.
    • The live demo uses one model and one pushback wording. The scorer and the added checks are exploratory, added after the preregistration.
